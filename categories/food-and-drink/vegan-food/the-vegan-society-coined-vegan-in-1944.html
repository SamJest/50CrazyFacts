The Vegan Society Coined “Vegan” in 1944

A single word changed food history forever—why 1944?

💥 Impact

Naming the movement unified disparate ethical eaters under one identity.

It shifted veganism from private practice to organized activism with global influence.

🤯 Did You Know?

The word “vegan” was invented in wartime Britain in 1944.

In 1944, during the chaos of World War II Britain, Donald Watson and a small group of non-dairy vegetarians decided vegetarianism wasn’t strict enough. They coined the word “vegan,” taking the first and last letters of “vegetarian” to signify “the beginning and end” of it. Imagine ration books, bomb sirens, and blackouts—yet amid this turmoil, a linguistic revolution in food ethics was born. Watson wasn’t chasing trends; he was formalizing a philosophy that rejected all animal exploitation. The early Vegan Society newsletters were typed, stapled, and mailed to just a few dozen members. Their meetings were modest, but their ambition was radical: redefine humanity’s relationship with food. At a time when meat was scarce anyway, their stance seemed both inconvenient and prophetic. The term “vegan” gave coherence to scattered plant-based practices stretching back centuries. One word transformed a dietary choice into a defined social movement.
