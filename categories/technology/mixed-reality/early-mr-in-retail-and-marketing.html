Early MR in Retail and Marketing

Your first MR ad probably appeared before you realized it.

💥 Impact

It’s one thing to make technology look cool; it’s another to make it affect behavior. MR in retail showed that evolution is driven as much by human psychology as by technical capability.

Retail MR illustrates that once MR leaves labs, it evolves to become practical, persuasive, and profit-driven.

🤯 Did You Know?

Mid-2010s MR apps let shoppers place furniture in their homes, changing buying decisions with digital overlays.

In the mid-2010s, brands began experimenting with MR for marketing, using smartphone cameras and tablets to overlay digital products in real-world spaces. IKEA pioneered this with its furniture app, letting users “place” sofas and tables in their living rooms before buying. This represented a shift from MR as novelty to MR as functional experience influencing consumer behavior. Marketers discovered that MR increased engagement, reduced purchase hesitation, and even generated social media buzz as users shared their augmented spaces online. These applications relied on the evolution of MR tracking, lighting adaptation, and realistic rendering to feel believable. Interestingly, consumer psychology played a central role: users reported a strange sense of “ownership” over virtual objects, sometimes reacting emotionally to furniture they knew wasn’t real. Retail MR exemplifies how technology adapts to economic needs, merging utility and entertainment in unprecedented ways. By combining spatial computing, graphics, and user behavior insights, MR evolved into a tool with measurable impact on daily life.
