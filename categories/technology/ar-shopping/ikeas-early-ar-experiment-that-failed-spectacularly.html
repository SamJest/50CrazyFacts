Ikea’s Early AR Experiment That Failed Spectacularly

Ikea almost killed AR shopping before it started—because of one tiny miscalculation.

💥 Impact

This misstep illustrates the messy reality of adopting new tech: initial failures can be just as influential as early successes. While the app didn’t sell more furniture at first, it familiarized millions with the idea of AR shopping.

AR shopping’s early viral failures created cultural awareness, showing that even glitches can drive adoption in a tech-hungry public.

🤯 Did You Know?

Floating furniture wasn’t just funny—it helped introduce an entire generation to the concept of augmented reality retail.

In 2013, Ikea launched an early AR app for tablets, letting users overlay furniture in their rooms. The idea was brilliant, but the execution was… chaotic. Users quickly discovered that if your room had poor lighting or weird angles, the app would make chairs float midair or sofas sink into walls. Social media exploded with screenshots of impossibly levitating furniture. The company had underestimated the limitations of mobile cameras and sensors, and early AR tracking algorithms were laughably error-prone. Yet, those “floating furniture” images became viral, unintentionally marketing AR shopping before it was ready. Developers scrambled to improve spatial recognition, lighting adjustments, and real-world mapping. By 2015, the app worked far better, but the early fiasco remains a reminder that AR shopping didn’t start smooth—it stumbled hilariously. The public, however, loved it, and the failures themselves helped normalize the concept of visualizing products digitally. Ikea learned that early tech warts could actually build hype.
