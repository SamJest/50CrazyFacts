The Accidental Birth of IoT in the 1980s

Before “IoT” was even a term, scientists plugged a Coke machine into the internet—why?

💥 Impact

In essence, this early experiment crystallized a radical notion: even mundane machines could join digital networks. The concept seemed silly then—why wire soda cans?—but it planted seeds for smart homes, industrial sensors, and even connected toasters. It was proof that convenience could be automated, and data could be collected everywhere.

The real innovation wasn’t the soda—it was the leap in thinking. Suddenly, researchers saw the potential for objects to report, measure, and act autonomously. The Coke machine was a whisper of a world where everything, from streetlights to toothbrushes, could have a digital voice.

🤯 Did You Know?

In 1982, a Coca-Cola machine at Carnegie Mellon University became the first device ever connected to the internet to report its status.

Imagine walking into a lab in 1982 and finding a Coca-Cola machine wired to the early internet, able to report which drinks were cold. Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University literally created one of the first “internet-connected devices” without calling it IoT. They wanted to save lab workers the embarrassment of shaking warm soda cans. Back then, modems were slower than a snail on sedatives, yet somehow the machine transmitted data over the ARPANET, the precursor to our modern internet. This early experiment unintentionally mapped the idea that everyday objects could “talk” to networks. While the world obsessed over email and file sharing, this Coke machine was quietly rewriting the rules of connectivity. Could you imagine if every appliance reported its status today? That modest Coke machine was, ironically, a prophet of a trillion-device future. And unlike our modern devices, it couldn’t be hacked for memes—though the concept was there. The team probably didn’t foresee people in 2026 controlling their fridges from space.
