The Smart Ring That Predates Bluetooth

Can a tiny ring hold secrets of wearable tech history? Absolutely.

💥 Impact

Invisible technology became a talking point decades before the Apple Watch made it mainstream, showing that wearable tech could be stylish, discreet, and functional. It forced designers to reconcile aesthetics with electronics—a lesson still vital today.

That tiny 1995 smart ring foretold a future where wearables could be both fashion statements and functional gadgets.

🤯 Did You Know?

A 1995 Japanese smart ring was an early wearable, sending data to computers before Bluetooth existed.

In 1995, a Japanese company released one of the first data-transmitting wearable devices in the form of a ring capable of sending infrared signals to computers. While it could store only a few kilobytes, this device foreshadowed the modern wearable obsession with minimalism and discreet functionality. Users could “click” information into computers, merging jewelry with computation in a pre-Bluetooth era. It looks quaint now, yet at the time it was futuristic enough to inspire sci-fi writers. Imagine trying to explain that a ring could act like a remote control for your PC in a world dominated by floppy disks. The device raised eyebrows at tech expos, creating both excitement and skepticism. It demonstrated that wearables didn’t need to be obvious or bulky to matter, challenging designers to consider elegance alongside utility. Its miniaturization paved the way for smart rings today, which monitor health, payments, and even biometric data. By blending fashion and tech, the ring highlighted the social dimensions of wearables long before Apple and Google entered the scene.
