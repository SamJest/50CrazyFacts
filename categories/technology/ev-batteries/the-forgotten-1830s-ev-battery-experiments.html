The Forgotten 1830s EV Battery Experiments

Before anyone drove an EV, inventors were tinkering with primitive “voltaic accumulators” in London labs.

💥 Impact

Though largely forgotten, Davidson’s experiments provided the first public proof that electric propulsion was possible. They also highlighted the critical problem of energy density, a challenge that engineers still wrestle with today. The idea that electricity could carry a heavy vehicle, even briefly, reshaped the perception of mobility for scientists and urban planners.

These early electric experiments also influenced early rail and tram design, with some cities considering electricity for urban transport decades before internal combustion engines took over. It’s a reminder that innovation often appears absurd before it becomes normal.

🤯 Did You Know?

Before EVs hit the streets, battery experiments in 1830s London created sparks, literally and figuratively, in science salons.

As early as 1834, a Scottish inventor named Robert Davidson built what some consider the world’s first electric locomotive, powered by primitive voltaic piles, which were essentially early batteries made of zinc and copper plates. His “battery” could only run for a few miles before needing a recharge, but it demonstrated that stored electricity could move heavy objects. Curiously, Davidson’s experiments caused a stir in Edinburgh and London salons, where scientists debated whether mechanical engines or electric currents would dominate the future. The battery technology of the 1830s was laughably inefficient by today’s standards—one locomotive required dozens of bulky cells and weighed more than a small tank—but it sparked imaginations. Early newspapers called it “an electrical carriage that runs without steam or horse,” though many readers assumed it was a hoax. Davidson himself reportedly powered small carriages in workshops, generating sparks and occasional smoke, leading to minor fires and a reputation for being a mad scientist. What’s astonishing is that even then, inventors understood the principles of rechargeable chemistry, laying groundwork for the 20th-century EV boom. His failed attempts weren’t failures at all—they were blueprints for the next generation of battery pioneers. In a way, Davidson’s “wild electric locomotive” was humanity’s first flirtation with green transport, long before climate change became a concern.
