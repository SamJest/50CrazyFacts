The Forgotten Solar Pioneer: Willoughby Smith

Who turned sunlight into electricity before most knew it could be done?

💥 Impact

Smith’s work underscores how accidental discoveries can redefine technology, linking telecommunications with energy science. It illustrates that solar innovation often stems from cross-disciplinary curiosity and experimentation.

Recognizing Smith reminds us that scientific progress is cumulative, built on forgotten breakthroughs that pave the way for major technological leaps.

🤯 Did You Know?

Willoughby Smith’s selenium experiments indirectly enabled the first functional solar cells by providing critical insight into photoconductivity.

In 1873, English engineer Willoughby Smith discovered the photoconductivity of selenium, realizing that its electrical resistance changed under sunlight exposure. This accidental breakthrough occurred while he was testing materials for telegraph cables, yet it became a cornerstone for early solar cells. Smith observed that selenium’s behavior was unpredictable yet repeatable when exposed to sunlight, hinting at the photovoltaic effect before Fritts’ gold-coated cells. His meticulous notes detailed variations in light intensity and resistance, essentially mapping the sun’s influence on electricity. Though largely forgotten in popular histories, Smith’s work provided critical empirical data that informed subsequent photovoltaic experiments. It’s fascinating to consider that solar power’s roots are entwined with telegraph technology—communications gave rise to energy innovation. Without Smith’s discovery, early solar pioneers like Fritts and Becquerel might have lacked key guidance on material behavior under sunlight. In short, one man’s curiosity about telegraph wires helped ignite a solar revolution centuries ahead of mass adoption.
