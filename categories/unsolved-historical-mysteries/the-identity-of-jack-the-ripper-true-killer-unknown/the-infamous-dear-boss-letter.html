The Infamous “Dear Boss” Letter

One letter may have named Jack the Ripper himself—but did he write it?

💥 Impact

The 'Dear Boss' letter exemplifies how communication can extend terror beyond physical acts. It forced police to consider both the content and source, complicating investigation. Public fascination increased, and the letter became a touchstone for fear and rumor. The creation of a moniker personalized the threat, making the killer a recognizable figure in society. It demonstrates early interactions between media, public perception, and criminal behavior. Psychologically, it highlights the potency of self-branding, even for criminals. Historically, it cemented the Ripper’s identity in culture and archives.

Culturally, the letter influenced subsequent portrayals of the Ripper in literature, theatre, and film. It shows how narrative can amplify notoriety, independent of actual actions. Modern criminology recognizes the strategic use of communication in controlling perception. Even today, the letter sparks debate over authenticity and intent. Its influence demonstrates that written words can become as terrifying as physical acts. The 'Dear Boss' letter bridges crime, media, and legend, highlighting how one document can shape centuries of fascination. Jack the Ripper’s identity owes as much to narrative invention as to reality.

🤯 Did You Know?

The 'Dear Boss' letter was the first to use the name 'Jack the Ripper,' creating the persona that haunts history.

The 'Dear Boss' letter, received by the Central News Agency in September 1888, introduced the infamous pseudonym 'Jack the Ripper.' It described future murders and mocked police efforts, suggesting inside knowledge or cunning foresight. Some experts argue it was written by the killer to taunt authorities and gain notoriety. Others claim it was a hoax by journalists or pranksters, designed to sell newspapers. Regardless of authorship, it shaped public perception and created a chilling personal identity for the murderer. The letter demonstrates the power of narrative to influence fear and attention. It also shows how a single piece of communication can become central to historical memory. By naming himself, whether authentic or fabricated, the killer—or hoaxer—ensured lasting infamy. The letter remains a pivotal artifact in the study of both crime and media influence.
