The Smallest Event Horizon Ever Calculated

How tiny can a black hole get before it disappears?

💥 Impact

Microscopic horizons push physics to extremes of density and energy.

They illustrate how event horizon physics scales from the tiniest primordial remnants to colossal black holes.

🤯 Did You Know?

Primordial black holes could have horizons smaller than atomic nuclei, evaporating via Hawking radiation.

Primordial black holes, theorized remnants of the early universe, could host event horizons smaller than an atomic nucleus. These microscopic horizons are speculative but predicted by inflationary models and quantum gravity. Their density is staggering—cramming a star’s mass into a nucleus-sized volume. At this scale, Hawking radiation dominates, potentially evaporating the black hole in microseconds. Unlike stellar-mass black holes, these mini horizons challenge our understanding of entropy, quantum fluctuations, and the limits of spacetime. Detecting such tiny horizons directly is currently impossible; we rely on indirect signatures like gamma-ray bursts or lensing anomalies. Despite their size, the physics at play mirrors their supermassive cousins, just in a high-energy, high-curvature regime. These tiny horizons highlight the universe’s incredible scale variance—from subatomic to galactic. They also bridge cosmology, particle physics, and quantum mechanics in a single theoretical framework.
