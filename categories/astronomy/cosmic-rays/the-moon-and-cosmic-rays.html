The Moon and Cosmic Rays

The Moon has its own cosmic signature.

💥 Impact

The Moon’s surface preserves cosmic ray history, offering insights into solar and galactic phenomena.

It turns the lunar surface into a natural particle detector for studying space radiation.

🤯 Did You Know?

Lunar rocks reveal cosmic ray exposure over millions of years, acting as natural detectors.

The Moon, lacking a protective atmosphere and magnetic field, is bombarded directly by cosmic rays. Over billions of years, this bombardment has implanted rare isotopes like Helium-3 and altered lunar soil chemistry. The tracks of cosmic rays in lunar rocks serve as natural detectors, preserving a record of cosmic ray flux through time. Lunar regolith samples brought back by Apollo missions reveal variations linked to solar activity, supernova events, and galactic cosmic ray density. This information provides clues about the history of our solar system’s neighborhood. Cosmic rays also contribute to the sputtering and slow erosion of the lunar surface, subtly shaping its topography. Studying lunar cosmic ray effects informs planning for future colonization, radiation shielding, and resource utilization. The Moon is, in a sense, a living cosmic diary. Each particle that strikes it records a chapter of the galaxy's energetic history.
