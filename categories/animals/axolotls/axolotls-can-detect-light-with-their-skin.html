Axolotls Can Detect Light With Their Skin

Eyes aren’t the only way axolotls sense their environment—they can “see” with their skin.

Axolotl skin contains photoreceptor cells that respond to light intensity. While not forming images like eyes, these cells help regulate behavior, such as hiding during bright sunlight or moving toward shaded areas. In shallow lakes, this helps them avoid predators and overheating. Skin photoreception may also influence circadian rhythms and feeding times. This dual sensory system—eyes and skin—gives axolotls an added layer of environmental awareness. Researchers study it to understand non-visual photoreception in vertebrates. It’s a quirky feature that seems almost science-fictional.

Why This Matters

Skin-based light detection improves survival and environmental adaptation.

It highlights the diversity of sensory systems in animals, offering insights into alternative ways to perceive the world.

Did You Know?

They can detect changes in light even under murky water conditions.

Source

Journal of Experimental Biology (jeb.biologists.org)
