Oldest Known Aye-Aye in Captivity

Lived up to 25 years, far exceeding typical wild lifespan of 20 years.

Aye-ayes are already long-lived for primates of their size, often reaching 20 years in the wild. In captivity, an individual lived to 25 years, setting the known longevity record. Factors included regular feeding, medical care, and lack of predators. Longevity studies show that aye-ayes age more slowly than similar-sized lemurs. Their nocturnal, low-energy lifestyle may contribute to lifespan extension. Researchers study old individuals for insights into aging, neural integrity, and dental wear patterns. Longer life provides more opportunities for learning and behavioral refinement. Such outliers inform both conservation and primatology. Understanding longevity extremes can guide captive management strategies. This also illustrates the importance of habitat stability for survival.

Why This Matters

Significance: Longevity records help researchers understand lifespan determinants and health strategies in small primates.

Extreme ages provide data on cumulative effects of diet, metabolism, and environment.

Did You Know?

The oldest recorded aye-aye had all adult incisors intact at death.

Source

[International Journal of Primatology, springer.com]
