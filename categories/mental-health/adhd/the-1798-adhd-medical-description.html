The 1798 ADHD Medical Description

ADHD was described in 1798—over a century before the term existed. The symptoms sound eerily familiar…

💥 Impact

Crichton’s observations demolish the modern myth that ADHD is a product of digital distraction or sugar-laced breakfasts. Physicians observing identical behavioral patterns in the 18th century show that ADHD predates TikTok by centuries. This continuity gives modern diagnostic frameworks historical validation, framing ADHD as a longstanding human variation rather than a cultural invention. It also underscores that attention differences have always existed and always required empathy.

Within mental health, this early documentation illustrates the slow shift from moral judgment to neurological understanding. Naming a condition can be the first step toward compassion, treatment, and societal acceptance. Crichton’s quill foreshadowed modern ADHD research.

🤯 Did You Know?

The first clinical description of ADHD symptoms appeared over 50 years before the stethoscope was widely used.

In 1798, Scottish physician Sir Alexander Crichton published a medical textbook describing children with an “incapacity of attending with a necessary degree of constancy.” He wasn’t talking about laziness, bad manners, or poor parenting. He described minds that were pulled toward every new stimulus “as if born with it,” long before modern neuroscience mapped dopamine pathways or executive function circuits. Crichton even noted the condition could persist into adulthood, contradicting the myth that hyperactive children simply “grow out of it.” Imagine diagnosing ADHD with powdered wigs and candlelight. There were no brain scans, no classroom behavior charts, just observation and careful prose. He emphasized that attentional instability was not a moral failure but a medical issue, a radical distinction in 18th-century medicine. ADHD didn’t emerge with smartphones; it was already being documented when Napoleon was still a lieutenant.
